What happens behind the scenes

No chatbot, no prompt, no generated text. One call to a model that only answers typed questions — and says how sure it is.

1 · Your message becomes "state"

The text you paste (and the sender, if you give one) is packaged as a small JSON object: { channel: "SMS received on a phone in India", message: "…", sender: "…" }. Nothing is rewritten or summarised; the model sees exactly what you saw.

2 · Four typed questions, one request

The state goes to Jev, TypeSafe's "System One" model, reached through OpenRouter. Jev doesn't write; it answers questions of three fixed kinds, all in parallel, with calibrated probabilities. An SMS is usually a scam and a WhatsApp forward is usually a false claim, so both sets are asked at once and a router question decides which half the verdict is about:

  • Is it a scam? — a yes/no question. Comes back as a probability, e.g. 0.86.
  • Which family? — a choice over ten labels (KYC, electricity, courier, lottery, fake job, bank/UPI, loan, impersonation, other scam, genuine), each described in a sentence. Comes back as a probability for every label.
  • How dangerous? — a score 0–4 against a written rubric, from "harmless" to "impersonates authority with threats to extract money". Comes back as a probability per level.
  • How creative? — a score 0–4, from "the template everyone has seen" to "would surprise a seasoned skeptic". Feeds the leaderboard.
  • What kind of message is this? — the router: scam, misinformation, both, or harmless.
  • Is it misleading? — yes/no, for claims about the world that are false, unverifiable or lifted out of their real context.
  • Which kind of claim? — a choice over ten: health myth, communal hoax, political, money rumour, recycled old media, doctored/AI media, chain forward, false alarm, other, none.
  • How much harm, and how hard does it push you to forward it? — two more scores, the second reading the "send to 10 people or else" machinery directly.

The questions and rubrics are plain text in the code — that's the whole "prompt". The call takes about a quarter of a second and costs around ₹0.002.

3 · The confidence gate

Because the answers are probabilities, the verdict can know when it's guessing. It's marked needs a human look when any of these hold:

  • the scam probability is within 0.25 of even (between 25% and 75%);
  • the family answer's confidence is under 0.5 — TypeSafe's own statistic for how concentrated the choice is (1.0 = all on one label; their guidance: below 0.5 "the model is genuinely unsure, don't guess");
  • the two questions disagree — "not a scam" overall, yet a scam family scores over 50%.

The gate follows the router: a WhatsApp health myth is not second-guessed for being a poor scam, and an electricity SMS is not quizzed on its factual accuracy. When the router itself is unsure which kind of message it is, both halves are judged — that uncertainty is exactly when a person should look. The thresholds are a handful of named numbers in the code.

4 · A human rules; both answers are kept

Flagged messages wait in the Review tab. A reviewer marks them scam or genuine and can correct the family. The ruling is stored next to the model's answer, never over it — so the stats can say how often the model agreed with people, and the review set slowly becomes a labelled dataset.

5 · The leaderboard

Only confirmed scams qualify: ruled a scam by a human, or called a scam by the model without a review flag. Ranked by the creativity score, then upvotes, then recency. Messages are fingerprinted with numbers and links stripped, so "seen 3× before" recognises a campaign even when every copy has a different phone number.

On your phone

Install it (the button on the Check tab, or Share → Add to Home Screen on iPhone) and Android registers it as a share target. After that, long-press any message in WhatsApp or your SMS app → Share → Scam Radar, and it opens with the text already in and the check already running. No copy-paste, no app store, no permissions — the page never reads your messages, you hand it one at a time.

What's stored, and what isn't

The message text, the sender if given, the timestamp, the model's full answer, any human ruling and the vote count — in a small SQLite database. No account, no cookies beyond the reviewer's own token, no phone number of yours. The numbers inside scam messages are the evidence and are kept. Code: github.com/marknaman05/scam-radar.
